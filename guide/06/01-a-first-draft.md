# A first draft, not a final source

<!-- index: 9#7, 9#8, 9#1, 9#2, 9#6 -->

Lesson 1 was about what ChatGPT gets wrong. This lesson is about what to
do with a response before it is relied on, and it begins where OpenAI's
own advice begins.

## OpenAI's rule

OpenAI's help-center page on whether ChatGPT tells the truth puts its
practical advice in one sentence: use ChatGPT as a first draft, not a
final source. The same page says that OpenAI encourages users to approach
ChatGPT critically and to verify important information from reliable
sources. Those are the vendor's words about its own product, and this
lesson takes them as its rule.

## Why a draft

The page's reason is the one lesson 1 set out. ChatGPT is designed to
provide useful responses based on patterns in the data it was trained on,
and, like any language model, it can produce incorrect or misleading
outputs. It may sound confident even when it is wrong, and the page lists
that separately among the model's limitations: confidence is not
reliability, and the model may express high confidence even in incorrect
answers. A first draft is what a document is before someone has checked
it. The page's phrase puts a response in that category and in no other.

Suppose a response says that a filing falls due on a particular date. On
the page's rule, that sentence is a draft of the fact until the date has
been found in the source that sets it, and how confidently the response
states it changes nothing.

## What a final source would be

The alternative the page names, a final source, is what a conclusion
rests on, and the page says a response is not to be used as one. What a
conclusion rests on instead is whatever the response has been checked
against, and the next two sections are about what to check and how.
Section 04 says what the AICPA Code requires of a member on the same
point, in the Code's own words, and sections 05 and 06 turn to who does
the checking and how a firm records it.

# Consent, disclosure, and the shape of the policy

<!-- index: 4#14, 4#15, 4#16, 1#12 -->

The toolkit's remaining practices are about the client and about the
policy's shape.

## Disclosure and consent

On sharing with a vendor, the toolkit says: if a client's confidential
information will be shared with a third party vendor, including AI,
provide written disclosure to and obtain specific consent from the
client in the appropriate format before the confidential information is
shared. Three things are in that sentence. The toolkit treats an AI
vendor as a third party vendor; the disclosure is written and the
consent is specific; and both come before the information is shared.
The toolkit cites no rule for the practice; its wording is close to the
Code's second route in section 02, and it is the toolkit's practice, not
the Code's requirement. On disclosure more generally, the toolkit adds:
if disclosure is not specifically required, consider still disclosing a
firm's use of AI in the engagement letter. That is a practice the
toolkit offers whether or not a rule requires it, and the toolkit does
not say which rules, if any, do.

## Ordinary and extraordinary use

On the shape of the policy, the toolkit says to define ordinary use for
AI tools, such as writing marketing language, versus extraordinary use
such as using AI to produce counsel or professional advice. The two
examples are the toolkit's, and it gives no further list. The
distinction gives a policy two tiers, and the toolkit places the
production of professional advice in the second.

## Why a written policy

Section 02 gave the sentence of the Code that bears on all of this: a
member would be considered in violation of the Rule if the member cannot
demonstrate that safeguards were applied that eliminated or reduced
significant threats to an acceptable level. A practice that exists only
in the habits of individual staff is hard to demonstrate; a written
policy that names what goes in, what is de-identified first, and when
the client is told and asked is a thing the member can show. Whether a
given firm's policy is enough is not something this course says.

## What this lesson has and has not said

It has said, in the Code's words, what confidential client information
is and what the Rule and its third-party interpretation require; in the
statute's words, what a New Hampshire licensee may not voluntarily
disclose and the one general release; as this course's position and
under its own heading, how the two rules bear on entering such
information into ChatGPT; and on the toolkit's authority, that client and business information stays
out of a public tool, that personal information is de-identified before
it enters any tool, that the client is told and asked before
confidential information is shared with an AI vendor, and that a policy
separates ordinary from extraordinary use. It has not said what any
other state's law provides, whether OpenAI's published controls amount
to reasonable assurance for any firm, whether a business workspace is a
public tool in the toolkit's sense, or what is permitted in any reader's
own situation. Lesson 6 shows one task carried through from start to
finish.

# Third-party service providers

<!-- index: 1#6, 1#7, 1#8, 1#9, 1#10, 1#11, 1#12, 1#16 -->

The Code has an interpretation for one common case: a member who uses
someone outside the firm to help provide professional services. The
Code's term for that outsider is a third-party service provider, and its
definitions give the term a wide meaning: an entity that the member does
not control, individually or collectively with his or her firm or with
members of his or her firm. Control is the test. A provider is defined
by whether the member controls it, not by what it does.

## The threat

The interpretation on the use of such providers opens by naming the
risk: when a member uses a third-party service provider to assist the
member in providing professional services, threats to compliance with
the Confidential Client Information Rule may exist. The Code's response
to a threat is a safeguard, and the interpretation gives a member two
ways to proceed before confidential client information goes to a
provider.

## The first route: a contract and reasonable assurance

The first is to enter into a contractual agreement with the third-party
service provider to maintain the confidentiality of the information and
provide reasonable assurance that the third-party service provider has
appropriate procedures in place. There are two things in that sentence:
the contract, and the assurance about the provider's procedures. On how
much work the assurance takes, the interpretation says it depends on
factors including the extent of publicly available information on the
third-party service provider's controls and procedures to safeguard
confidential client information. A provider that publishes information
about its own controls has, on the Code's account, given the member part
of what the member needs in order to assess it. Lesson 2 described the
documents OpenAI publishes about its business plans; whether they amount
to reasonable assurance for a given firm is not something this course
says.

## The second route: the client's specific consent

The other is to obtain specific consent from the client before
disclosing confidential client information to the third-party service
provider. The interpretation says what makes consent specific: the
consent should specify the nature of the information that may be
disclosed, the type of third party to whom it may be disclosed, and its
intended use. Three things, then: what, to whom, and for what. Consent
that names none of the three is not the consent the interpretation
describes.

## What stays with the member

Neither route ends the member's responsibility. The Rule's
interpretation says that a member would be considered in violation of
the Confidential Client Information Rule if the member cannot
demonstrate that safeguards were applied that eliminated or reduced
significant threats to an acceptable level. What the member has to be
able to do is demonstrate it, which is a record as much as an act. The
General Standards Rule's own interpretation on third-party providers,
which lesson 4 gave, adds the other half: the member must also obtain
sufficient relevant data to support the work product and comply with all
technical standards applicable to the professional services. Using a
provider changes who does part of the work; it does not change who
answers for it. Whether OpenAI is a third-party service provider in the
Code's sense is a question the Code does not answer, and section 04 says
what this course makes of it.
